The Functional Consultant

The Odoo product expert who turns requirements into a configured, working system. The Functional Consultant (FC) stands between the business (what the Business Analyst defined) and the technical team (the developers who close true gaps) — and their deliverable is not a document but a solution that runs. If the PM owns certainty and the BA owns clarity, the FC owns fit: making real Odoo do the real job.

Module 1

What the role really is

A Functional Consultant is the person who makes Odoo actually do what the client needs. The BA has captured what the business needs; the developers can build whatever is truly missing. In between sits the FC, who answers the single question that defines the role: "How does Odoo deliver this — and how much of it does standard Odoo already do?"

The FC's product is a working solution. Not a requirements document, not a Gantt chart, not lines of code — a configured Odoo database where a salesperson can quote, a warehouse can ship, and finance can close the month. Everything the FC does ladders up to that running system.

On an Odoo implementation the FC is the connective tissue between three worlds:

  • The business need — the prioritized, testable requirements handed over by the Business Analyst.
  • The Odoo platform — its apps, its data model, its standard behaviour, and its limits. The FC knows what Odoo does out of the box better than anyone on the project.
  • The development team — to whom the FC hands a precise functional spec for the genuine gaps that configuration cannot close.
The FC is the only role that must be deeply fluent in the product itself. A PM can run an Odoo project knowing Odoo broadly; the FC cannot configure a chart of accounts, a warehouse, or a sales workflow without knowing exactly how Odoo models them.
Definition

A Functional Consultant translates business requirements into a configured ERP solution, using the product's standard capabilities first and specifying custom development only for genuine gaps. In Odoo terms the FC is the application expert across the integrated suite — the bridge between the requirement and the running database.

Module 2

The FC mindset

Before the mechanics, the operating instincts. Strong Odoo FCs think in a recognisable way:

Fit-to-standard first

The default answer is "standard Odoo can do this." The FC reaches for a setting or a standard feature before a custom field, and a custom field before custom code. Every deviation from standard is a cost the FC must justify, not a freedom to enjoy.

Configure before customize

There is a spectrum from a checkbox in Settings to a bespoke Python module. The FC always moves up that spectrum reluctantly, because everything to the left survives upgrades cleanly and everything to the right carries lifetime maintenance.

Think in the data model

Good FCs see Odoo as objects and flows: partners, products, orders, moves, journal entries. They ask "what record does this create, and what does it trigger downstream?" rather than "which button does the user press?"

Demo-driven

The FC believes nothing is agreed until the client has seen it work. They configure in small increments and show them early, because a five-minute demo surfaces misunderstandings that a fifty-page document hides.

Pragmatic, not purist

The best Odoo solution is rarely the most elegant one; it's the one the users will actually adopt within the budget. The FC trades theoretical perfection for fit, adoption, and upgradeability.

Process-aware

The FC doesn't configure a field in isolation; they configure a flow. Changing how a sales order is confirmed touches delivery, invoicing, and accounting. They hold the whole document chain in mind.

Module 4

How Odoo actually works — the FC's mental model

This is the conceptual backbone of the role. An FC who understands Odoo's architecture configures fast and correctly; one who doesn't fights the system at every turn.

One integrated database

The defining principle of Odoo is that all apps share a single database. The customer a salesperson creates in CRM is the same partner record finance invoices and the warehouse ships to. There is no syncing between modules because there is nothing to sync — it is one model. This is why "fit-to-standard" matters: the integration is free and automatic when you stay inside the standard objects.

Master data

A handful of records underpin everything. Getting these right is the foundation of a clean implementation:

  • Partners (res.partner) — customers, vendors, contacts, even companies and employees' related contacts. One partner can be a customer and a vendor at once.
  • Products (product.template / product.product) — with a type (storable, consumable, service), a category that drives accounting and inventory valuation, units of measure, and the routes that decide how they're procured.
  • Chart of accounts — the accounts, taxes, journals, and fiscal positions that determine how every transaction is booked.

Document flows

Odoo operates through chains of documents that trigger one another. The two canonical flows every FC must know cold:

Sell (Order-to-Cash)

Sales Order → Delivery → Customer Invoice → Payment. Confirming a sales order reserves stock and creates a delivery in Inventory; validating the delivery creates outgoing stock moves; the invoice draws from the order, and registering payment closes the loop and reconciles the bank.

Buy (Procure-to-Pay)

Purchase Order → Receipt → Vendor Bill → Payment. Confirming a PO creates an expected receipt; validating the receipt increases stock and (in real-time valuation) posts an inventory journal entry; the vendor bill is checked against the PO and receipt (three-way match) before payment.

Journals & stock moves — the invisible engine

Behind those friendly documents, two mechanisms do the real accounting work. Stock moves record every change in inventory location (including virtual locations like "Customers" and "Vendors"), giving a complete audit of where goods went. Journal entries are generated automatically — a validated delivery with real-time valuation, a confirmed invoice, a registered payment each post to the relevant journal. The FC must understand this so that a configuration choice (a product category's valuation method, a journal's default accounts) produces the right numbers in the financial reports.

Multi-company & multi-currency

Odoo supports multiple companies in one database with inter-company rules, and multiple currencies with automatic rate handling and conversion. The FC decides whether a client's group structure needs separate companies (separate ledgers, inter-company transactions) or can live as branches/analytic distinctions — a decision with large downstream consequences.

ℹ️ Why this matters

Because the apps share one database and drive each other through these flows, the FC can rarely change one thing in isolation. Configuring how stock is valued affects the P&L; changing a sales workflow affects when revenue is recognised. Holding the whole chain in mind is the difference between a senior FC and a button-clicker.

Module 5

Configuration vs. customization

The single most consequential judgment the FC makes, repeatedly, is where on the spectrum a given requirement should be met. There are three broad bands, and the FC's instinct is always to stay as far left as possible.

BandWhat it coversWho does itUpgrade cost
1. Configuration (settings)Activating apps and features, settings toggles, defining master data, pricelists, journals, workflows, access rights, email/report templates.FCNone — fully upgrade-safe.
2. Studio (low-code)Custom fields, custom models, view edits, automated actions, approval rules, PDF report tweaks, simple custom apps — all without writing code.FCLow — Studio changes are designed to carry across upgrades, within limits.
3. Developer customizationCustom modules: Python business logic, XML views, OWL front-end components, integrations, complex automations.Developer (from FC's spec)Highest — bespoke code must be tested and often reworked on every major upgrade.

The decision framework

  • Can a setting or standard feature do it? Use it. (Often the requirement only seemed custom.)
  • Does it need an extra field, a tweaked view, or a simple rule? Reach for Studio.
  • Does it need real business logic, a complex algorithm, or an external integration? Write a functional spec and hand it to a developer.
  • Could a small change to the client's process remove the need entirely? Always ask this first — the cheapest customization is the one you don't build.
⚠️ Over-customization is a slow trap

Every line of custom code is a permanent liability. It must be maintained, re-tested on each upgrade, and can break when Odoo changes its internals. A heavily customized Odoo eventually becomes un-upgradeable — clients get stranded on an old version, losing security patches and new features. "Configure first, customize last" is not dogma; it is what keeps the client able to take next year's Odoo release. When a client insists on bending the system to match an old habit, the FC's job is to ask whether the habit, not the software, should change.

Module 6

Solution design — the FC's signature deliverable

Solution design is where the FC earns the title. Taking the BA's prioritized requirements, the FC produces the solution design: the blueprint for how Odoo will deliver each one. It is the FC's signature artifact and the input to everything that follows.

A complete Odoo solution design covers:

Module selection

Which Odoo apps are in scope, and explicitly which are not. The map from business areas to apps — and the integrations between them.

Configuration approach

How each in-scope app will be set up: the key settings, the standard features used, and the workflows enabled. The "how standard Odoo meets the need" narrative.

Master-data design

The structure of partners, the product catalogue and categories, units of measure, and the coding conventions the client will live with for years.

Chart of accounts

The account structure, tax setup, journals, fiscal positions, and analytic structure — designed with the client's finance team, because it's the hardest thing to change later.

Workflows & approval rules

Where the business needs approval gates (purchase limits, discount thresholds, leave approvals) and how Odoo's standard or Studio approvals will enforce them.

Functional specs for gaps

For the true gaps that configuration can't close, the precise specifications handed to developers — covered in depth in Module 10.

⚙️ The TechNext angle

At TechNext, solution design rarely starts from a blank page. The FC begins from the vertical template for the client's industry — a pre-built configuration that already represents "standard Odoo for retail / manufacturing / services." Design then becomes a focused exercise: confirm the 80% the template already covers, and design only the 20% that is genuinely this client's own. The FC's judgment is concentrated exactly where it adds the most value.

Module 7

The FC across the eight phases

This is the core of the course. Here is exactly what the FC does in each phase of the Odoo lifecycle. The FC is consulted in discovery, leads design, build, configuration, functional testing and training, and is hands-on through hypercare.

1

Initiation & kickoff

Consulted. The FC contributes Odoo feasibility reality to the charter — flagging early which goals are clearly standard, which smell like customization, and which Odoo edition or apps the scope implies. They help size the work so the PM's plan is grounded in what Odoo can actually do.

2

Discovery & analysis

Consulted, often co-leading the fit-gap. While the BA elicits the as-is and to-be processes, the FC runs requirements against Odoo: for each one, is it standard, a configuration, a Studio tweak, or a true gap? The FC owns the fit-gap analysis — the most valuable output of discovery — because only they know the product well enough to make the call.

3

Solution design

Leads. The FC produces the solution design (Module 6): module selection, configuration approach, master-data and chart-of-accounts design, workflows, and the functional specs for developers. This is the FC's primary phase and signature deliverable.

4

Build & configuration

Leads. The FC configures Odoo in the staging environment — apps, settings, master data, workflows, pricelists, taxes, access rights, templates — and assembles the working solution. They drive the configure-demo-refine cadence and coordinate developers building the approved customizations to their specs.

5

Data migration

Supports and validates. The BA usually owns the data mapping; the FC owns the load and validation — using Odoo's import framework, loading data into the right models in the right order (partners and products before transactions), and reconciling that opening balances, stock, and open documents are correct in Odoo.

6

Testing & UAT

Leads functional testing. The FC writes and executes functional test scripts that walk the real business processes end-to-end, supports the client's UAT, and is the person who fixes configuration defects as they surface. They triage with the PM which defects are config fixes (theirs) versus code fixes (the developer's) versus genuine new scope.

7

Training & go-live

Delivers training. The FC trains key users and end users on the configured system, often via train-the-trainer, and produces the user documentation. At cutover they support the PM's runbook — validating the final data load and confirming the live system behaves as tested.

8

Hypercare & support

Leads early-life support. The FC is the front line for "how do I…" and "this isn't working" in the first weeks, resolving configuration issues fast, coaching users out of confusion, and feeding recurring questions back into the documentation and templates before handover to steady-state support.

Module 8

Configuration deep dive — what "configuring Odoo" really means

"Configuration" sounds vague until you see the concrete work. Here is the substance of what the FC sets up to turn a blank Odoo into a client's running system.

AreaWhat the FC configures
AccountingChart of accounts, journals (sales, purchase, bank, cash, miscellaneous), tax rules and tax groups, fiscal positions, payment terms, bank accounts and reconciliation models.
ProductsProduct categories (which drive income/expense accounts and stock valuation), units of measure, product attributes & variants, and the routes that decide procurement (buy, manufacture, make-to-order, dropship).
InventoryWarehouses and locations, operation types (receipts, deliveries, internal & manufacturing transfers), reordering rules, storage strategies, lots/serial tracking, and valuation method (standard / FIFO / average).
Sales & PurchasePricelists (rules, discounts, currency), quotation templates, the sales workflow (delivery & invoicing policy), and the purchase workflow (RFQ, vendor pricelists, reordering).
Approval rulesDiscount-limit, purchase-amount, expense, and leave approvals — enforced via standard settings or Studio approval rules with the right approver hierarchy.
CommunicationsEmail templates (order confirmations, invoices, reminders), automated follow-ups, and the outgoing/incoming mail servers.
DocumentsReport layouts and branding (logo, colours, headers/footers), customized invoice and delivery-slip layouts, and document numbering sequences.
SecurityUser roles, access rights (which groups see which apps and actions), and record rules (which records a user can see — e.g. salespeople see only their own opportunities, or a branch sees only its own warehouse).

A concrete example — setting up "sell from stock"

  • Create the product as storable, in a category whose accounting maps revenue and COGS correctly.
  • Set its route to Buy and define a reordering rule so the warehouse replenishes automatically.
  • Build a pricelist for the customer segment with the agreed discounts.
  • Configure the sales workflow: deliver then invoice, with a quotation template carrying the right terms.
  • Tune the delivery operation type and the invoice layout, then test the full Order → Delivery → Invoice → Payment flow before demoing it.

None of this is code. All of it is the FC's craft — and all of it survives the next Odoo upgrade untouched.

Module 9

Odoo Studio & low-code

Odoo Studio is the FC's power tool: it extends Odoo without code, and it sits in band 2 of the configuration spectrum. Knowing Studio's reach — and its limits — is what lets a good FC close most "gaps" without ever booking developer time.

What Studio can do

  • Custom fields on existing models, of every type (text, selection, many2one relations, computed-by-formula).
  • Custom models — entirely new objects with their own menus and views, for data Odoo doesn't track natively.
  • View editing — drag-and-drop changes to forms, lists, kanban, and the addition of tabs, groups, and buttons.
  • Automated actions — trigger an update, email, or activity when a record is created or changed.
  • Approval rules — gate an action behind one or more approvers.
  • PDF report editing — adjust and rebrand printed documents.
  • Simple apps — build a small standalone app from scratch, no developer required.

Where Studio stops

  • Complex business logic — multi-step algorithms, intricate validations.
  • External integrations — calling and syncing with third-party APIs.
  • Heavy performance-sensitive operations on large datasets.
  • Custom front-end / OWL components and bespoke interactive UI.
  • Anything requiring fine control over the ORM or transactional behaviour.

When a requirement crosses these lines, the FC stops configuring and starts specifying — see Module 10.

💡 The Studio judgment call

Studio is seductive — almost anything looks buildable. The discipline is restraint: a custom model duplicating something standard Odoo nearly does is technical debt with a friendly face. Use Studio for the genuine small gap; escalate the genuine large one. And document every Studio change, because invisible customizations are the ones that surprise the next person on the project.

Module 10

Writing functional specs for developers

When configuration and Studio can't close a gap, the FC writes a functional specification and hands it to a developer. Per the methodology, the FC → developer handoff is the most expensive ambiguity on the project: a vague spec becomes the wrong build, discovered only at UAT, paid for twice. The FC's spec-writing is therefore a core skill, not an afterthought.

What a good functional spec contains

  • The business need — why this matters, in the client's terms, so the developer understands intent, not just instructions.
  • The exact behaviour — precisely what should happen, step by step, including what happens on the standard path.
  • The data — which models and fields are involved, what's stored, computed, or related, and any new fields required.
  • The screens — where it appears in the UI: which views, buttons, menus, and what the user sees and does.
  • The edge cases — the unhappy paths: empty values, partial quantities, cancellations, multi-company, permissions. Where specs are usually thin and bugs are usually born.
  • Acceptance criteria — the testable conditions that prove it works, written before the build so "done" is unambiguous.
💡 Spec quality is the cheapest insurance on the project

An hour the FC spends sharpening a spec saves days of rework. The test of a good spec: a developer who has never met the client can build the right thing from it once, and the FC can verify it against the acceptance criteria without a conversation. If the spec needs a meeting to interpret, it isn't finished.

Module 11

Demos & the configure-demo-refine loop

The FC's working rhythm is iterative: configure a slice, demo it, capture feedback, refine, repeat. The demo is not a formality — it is the FC's primary instrument for confirming fit and catching misunderstandings while they're still cheap to fix.

How to demo Odoo well

  • Demo the process, not the screens. Walk a real scenario — "a customer orders, we ship, we invoice" — using the client's own products and language, not generic demo data.
  • Show working software early, while it's small. A rough demo this week beats a polished one a month from now.
  • Capture feedback as it lands — log every comment as a task, separating genuine corrections from new requests.
  • Manage expectations — be explicit about what's configured versus still to come, so users don't mistake a gap for a defect.
⚠️ Demos are where scope creep is born

The most dangerous sentence in a demo is "oh, and while you're in there, could it also…". Each request is reasonable; the sum blows the budget. The FC notes it, thanks them, and routes anything beyond the agreed scope to the PM's change control — never silently building it because the demo made it feel free. Distinguishing a legitimate correction from a new requirement, live, is a senior FC skill.

⚙️ The TechNext angle

TechNext FCs demo from pre-built industry demo instances — a retail or manufacturing Odoo already populated with realistic data. From day one the client sees something that looks like their business, which makes feedback concrete and accelerates the configure-demo-refine loop. The demo stops being "imagine this with your data" and becomes "here is roughly your business, what's different?"

Module 12

Testing & training

Two FC-led activities turn a configured system into an adopted one: proving it works, then teaching people to use it.

Functional testing & UAT

The FC writes functional test scripts that exercise the real business processes end-to-end — not "click this field" but "process a customer order from quote to paid invoice." During UAT the FC supports the client's testers, triages every defect, and fixes the configuration defects directly. The discipline is separating config fixes (FC), code fixes (developer), and disguised new scope (PM's change control) — and never quietly absorbing the third.

Training & documentation

The FC trains key users (who become internal champions) and end users, frequently via train-the-trainer so the client can sustain knowledge after go-live. They produce the user documentation — process guides written in the client's language, anchored to the configured system rather than generic Odoo manuals.

⚙️ The TechNext angle

At TechNext, AI drafts the first version of both test scripts and training material directly from the requirements and the configured system — covering the standard paths automatically. The FC's job shifts from writing them from scratch to validating and tailoring them: adding the client-specific edge cases the AI can't know and confirming the scripts actually reflect this client's processes. Faster to draft, still human-verified where it counts.

Module 14

Skills & KPIs that matter

The FC is the most product-technical of the three core roles, but the soft skills decide whether the solution is adopted. The hard skills get the configuration right; the soft skills get the client to trust and use it.

Hard skills

Deep Odoo functional knowledge across modules Accounting fundamentals Business process knowledge Studio / low-code Data import & mapping Basic technical literacy (to talk to devs) Functional testing

Soft skills (the decisive ones)

Client-facing presence Demoing & presentation Translating between business & tech Patience in training Problem-solving Active listening

An FC must understand accounting well enough to design a chart of accounts and explain why an entry posted as it did; they must understand enough technical detail to write a spec a developer can build from — without needing to write the code themselves. Enough technical literacy to brief and challenge a developer is the bar, not full developer skill.

Module 15

Boundaries & the TechNext lean model

Knowing where the FC stops is as important as knowing what they do.

  • FC vs. BA: the BA defines what the business needs; the FC decides how Odoo meets it. The BA owns the requirement; the FC owns the solution. The line is genuinely fuzzy — fit-gap analysis lives on the boundary — which is why TechNext often merges them into a single Solutions Engineer / Delivery Specialist seat in its A-Team.
  • FC vs. developer: the FC configures and specifies; the developer codes. The FC should never need to write production Python to deliver a project — when they reach for code, they should be writing a spec instead.
  • FC vs. PM: the FC makes the solution-design calls; the PM owns the schedule, budget, and the trade-off decisions. The FC tells the PM what a customization will take; the PM decides whether it's worth it.
⚙️ How the role changes in TechNext's A-Team

In TechNext's lean, automation-first model the FC's repeatable configuration stops being manual clicking and becomes vertical templates and config-as-code — assembled and applied at speed. Test scripts and training material are AI-drafted; demos start from pre-built industry instances. What stays irreducibly human is the solution-design judgment for the novel 20%: deciding how this client's genuinely unusual requirement should meet Odoo, where to draw the configure-vs-customize line, and how to keep the system upgradeable. Automate the configuration; never automate the design judgment.
