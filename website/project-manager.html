The Project Manager

The owner of the outcome. The PM is the single person accountable for delivering the agreed Odoo system to the agreed scope, on time, on budget, and at acceptable risk — while keeping the client confident and the team unblocked. They rarely touch Odoo themselves; their product is certainty.

Module 1

What the role really is

A Project Manager in an ERP context is not a note-taker who chases status. The PM is the person the client sponsor and the TechNext leadership both hold responsible when they ask one question: "Will this go live, as promised, without blowing up?" Everything the PM does ladders up to answering that question honestly and then making the answer "yes."

On an Odoo implementation specifically, the PM is the connective tissue between three worlds that naturally pull apart:

  • The client's business reality — operations that must keep running while the project happens, a budget that's real, and political dynamics between departments.
  • The delivery team — the Business Analyst, Functional Consultant, and developers, who need clear priorities and protection from churn.
  • The Odoo platform itself — its release cycle, its standard capabilities, its limits, and (for hosted projects) the Odoo.sh or Odoo Online environment.
The PM's deliverable is not a Gantt chart. It is a go-live the client trusts, reached without surprises. The chart is just one instrument on the dashboard.
Definition

Project Management is the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to project activities to meet the project requirements — balancing the competing demands of scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, and risk. In ERP, add a seventh constraint that dominates the rest: business change adoption. A perfectly configured Odoo that nobody uses is a failed project.

Module 2

The PM mindset

Before the mechanics, the mental model. Strong ERP PMs share a handful of operating instincts:

Outcome over activity

"We held 12 meetings" is not progress. "The data migration is signed off" is. The PM measures the project in completed, verifiable outcomes, not motion.

Surface bad news early

A slipping date discovered in week 2 is a planning adjustment. The same slip discovered the week before go-live is a crisis. Good PMs are professionally pessimistic and reward early warnings.

Protect the team's focus

The PM absorbs ambiguity, politics, and scope pressure so the FC and BA can do deep work. A PM who passes chaos straight through to the team isn't managing — they're forwarding.

Everything is a trade-off

You cannot add scope without touching time, cost, or quality. The PM's job is to make those trade-offs explicit and get the right person to decide them — never to silently absorb them.

Module 4

The PM across the eight phases

This is the core of the course. Here is exactly what the PM does in each phase of the Odoo lifecycle.

1

Initiation & kickoff

Write the project charter: objectives, success criteria, scope baseline, high-level timeline, budget, governance, and roles. Confirm the executive sponsor. Run the kickoff meeting where the client and TechNext agree on what "done" means. Establish the communication and escalation plan. Get the definition of success in writing before any work starts — this document is what you'll point to when scope creep arrives.

2

Discovery & analysis

The BA leads the work; the PM owns the outcome. Schedule the discovery workshops, make sure the right client people attend, and keep discovery time-boxed (it can sprawl forever). Watch for scope expanding silently as new requirements surface, and route every new ask through the change process. Begin the risk register from what discovery reveals.

3

Solution design

As the FC designs the Odoo solution, the PM converts it into a plan: what gets configured, what gets developed, what gets integrated, in what order, by whom, by when. Lock the scope baseline. Get formal client sign-off on the design and the SOW so that later "but I assumed…" conversations have a reference point.

4

Build & configuration

Run the delivery cadence — typically short configure-demo-refine cycles. Track progress against plan, remove blockers daily, manage the developer queue for any customizations, and keep the client informed with regular demos so there are no surprises at UAT. Guard the change log: every approved change updates scope, time, and cost.

5

Data migration

Often the riskiest phase and the one PMs underestimate. Own the migration plan and timeline: who extracts, who cleans, who maps, who validates, and the cutover sequencing. Schedule at least one full trial migration before go-live. Make data quality the client's responsibility explicit and early — dirty source data is the most common go-live delay.

6

Testing & UAT

Plan and chase User Acceptance Testing. Ensure test scenarios cover real business processes, that client testers actually show up, and that defects are logged, triaged, and tracked to closure. Hold the formal acceptance gate: no go-live without signed UAT. Negotiate which defects are go-live blockers vs. fix-in-hypercare.

7

Training & go-live

Own the cutover plan — the hour-by-hour runbook for the switch: final data load, validation checks, sign-offs, and a go/no-go decision with named criteria. Confirm users are trained. Decide the cutover strategy (big-bang vs. phased) with the client. On go-live day the PM runs the room: executes the runbook, communicates status, and owns the call to proceed or roll back.

8

Hypercare & support

Stand up intensive early-life support: a clear channel for issues, fast triage, and daily check-ins for the first week or two. Track issue volume trending down as the signal to exit hypercare. Run the project closure: final acceptance, lessons-learned review, handover to steady-state support, and the commercial close-out.

Module 5

Deliverables & artifacts

These are the tangible documents the PM produces and maintains. Each one exists to answer a recurring question before it's asked.

Project Charter

The founding document: why, what success looks like, scope, budget, timeline, sponsor, governance. Signed before work begins.

Project Plan / Schedule

Phases, tasks, dependencies, durations, milestones, owners. Maintained as a living Gantt, not a one-time artifact.

RACI matrix

Who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed for each major activity — removes "I thought you were doing that."

RAID log

Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies — the PM's situational-awareness dashboard, reviewed every week.

Status report

Weekly: % complete, what's done, what's next, budget burn, top risks, decisions needed. Same format every week so trends are visible.

Change request log

Every scope change with its impact on time/cost/quality and the approval. The PM's defense against scope creep.

Communication plan

Who hears what, how often, through which channel, and the escalation path when something's stuck.

Go-live / cutover runbook

The hour-by-hour checklist for switch-over day, including go/no-go criteria and the rollback plan.

Lessons-learned report

Closure document feeding the next project — at TechNext this feeds the knowledge base and improves the templates.

Module 6

Scope, time & cost — the iron triangle in depth

The three classic constraints are linked: change one and at least one other must move. The PM's craft is making that relationship visible to whoever is asking for the change.

Scope management

Scope is defined in the SOW and design sign-off. The discipline is the change control process: any new requirement is written up, its impact on time and cost is estimated, and the client sponsor decides whether to approve it (and pay/wait for it). The deadly anti-pattern is the "small favor" — a dozen unlogged small changes that quietly consume the budget and the timeline.

⚠️ Scope creep on ERP projects

ERP scope creep is rarely one big request. It's "can we also just add this field / this report / this approval step" repeated fifty times. Each is reasonable; the sum is a blown budget. The PM's job is to log every one and make the cumulative cost visible — not to be the "no" person, but to make trade-offs honest.

Schedule management

Build the schedule backward from constraints: the client's hard go-live driver (fiscal year, contract end, season), their blackout periods, and the team's real availability. Identify the critical path — the chain of dependent tasks that determines the end date. On Odoo projects the critical path usually runs through data migration and UAT, not configuration.

Cost management

For TechNext's productized fixed-price packages, "cost" management is really margin protection: tracking consulting days consumed vs. days sold, and catching the moment a project starts eating its own profit. The PM is the early-warning system for an engagement going underwater.

Module 7

Risk & issue management

A risk is something that might happen; an issue is a risk that has happened. The PM manages both, but the value is in the risk work — handling things before they become issues.

The risk process

  • Identify — brainstorm what could go wrong, continuously, with the team.
  • Assess — rate each by probability × impact to prioritize attention.
  • Plan a response — avoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept each one, with a named owner.
  • Monitor — review the register every week; risks change as the project moves.

The usual suspects on an Odoo implementation

RiskTypical mitigation
Dirty / incomplete legacy dataStart data cleansing early; make it the client's owned task; run a trial migration well before cutover.
Key users unavailable (busy with day jobs)Secure named time commitments in the charter; escalate to the sponsor when attendance slips.
Requirements that really need custom devSurface in design, not build; challenge whether standard Odoo + a process change can avoid the customization.
Low user adoption / change resistanceInvolve users early; invest in training and communication; identify and convert skeptics.
Scope creepStrict change control; visible cumulative impact.
Integration with a third-party system slipsIdentify integration dependencies on day one; get the third party committed to dates in writing.
Module 8

Stakeholder & change management

ERP projects are change projects. The technology usually works; the rollout fails when people don't adopt it. The PM (with the sponsor) owns the human side.

Map the stakeholders

Classify everyone by influence and interest. The high-influence/high-interest sponsor gets managed closely; the low-interest finance clerk gets kept informed. The dangerous quadrant is high-influence/low-interest people who can veto adoption without engaging — the PM brings them in deliberately.

The sponsor relationship

The executive sponsor is the PM's most important relationship. They unblock resources, make the decisions above the PM's authority, and carry the message to the rest of the business. A disengaged sponsor is itself a top risk — the PM works actively to keep them engaged and informed.

💡 Pro tip

Find the influential skeptic early — often a long-tenured supervisor who built the current way of working. Win them over and they become your best change agent. Ignore them and they'll quietly campaign against the new system from inside.

Module 10

Skills that matter

ERP project management is unusually weighted toward soft skills — the hard mechanics can be learned in weeks; the judgment takes years.

Hard skills

Planning & scheduling Budgeting & estimation Risk management Scope / change control Working knowledge of Odoo & ERP concepts Agile & Waterfall methods Reporting & metrics

Soft skills (the decisive ones)

Communication & facilitation Stakeholder influence Negotiation Conflict resolution Decisiveness under pressure Calm in a crisis Empathy for the client's change

A PM does not need to be able to configure Odoo — but they must understand it well enough to call a bad estimate, challenge an unnecessary customization, and translate between the FC and the client. Enough ERP literacy to ask the right hard questions is the bar.

Module 12

A week in the life

An illustrative week for a TechNext PM running two concurrent Odoo implementations in the build phase.

MON
Review the RAID log for both projects; send the weekly status reports; confirm the week's demos are scheduled and the right client people will attend.
TUE
Internal stand-up with the FC and BA — what's blocked, what's at risk. Chase the client on the data extract that's three days late (now a flagged risk).
WED
Run the configure-demo session with Client A; capture feedback as tasks, not scope changes — and route the two real new requests through change control.
THU
Plan the upcoming UAT for Client B: build the test schedule, confirm testers, walk the FC through the acceptance gate. Update the cutover runbook draft.
FRI
Sponsor check-in calls; update both Gantts and budget burn; triage the week's defects; write next week's priorities. Decide nothing is on fire over the weekend.
Module 13

Common pitfalls — and how to avoid them

❌ Letting discovery sprawl

Open-ended requirements gathering with no time-box. Fix: hard-limit discovery, and push undecided items to a parking lot with owners and dates.

❌ Treating data migration as an afterthought

Leaving it to the last sprint. Fix: start data work in parallel with config; run a trial load early; make data quality the client's owned task from day one.

❌ Saying yes to "small" changes

Unlogged favors that compound. Fix: every change goes through the log with its time/cost impact visible.

❌ Skipping or rushing UAT

Going live on the FC's confidence instead of the client's sign-off. Fix: a real acceptance gate with named testers and signed approval.

❌ Under-investing in training & change

A perfect system nobody adopts. Fix: budget training and communication as real deliverables, not a final-week add-on.

❌ Reporting green until it's suddenly red

Optimism that hides slippage until it's too late to fix. Fix: honest status, early bad news, and a culture that rewards the warning.

Module 14

Boundaries & the TechNext lean model

Knowing where the PM stops is as important as knowing what they do.

  • PM vs. BA: the BA decides what the business needs; the PM decides when it gets delivered and at what cost. The PM doesn't write requirements; the BA doesn't manage the budget.
  • PM vs. FC: the FC decides how Odoo will solve it; the PM doesn't make solution-design calls. But the PM does challenge whether a customization is worth its time and cost.
  • PM vs. sponsor: the sponsor makes the business decisions above the PM's authority (extra budget, scope trade-offs, go/no-go); the PM frames those decisions clearly and recommends.
⚙️ How the role changes in TechNext's A-Team

In TechNext's lean, automation-first model there is rarely a dedicated PM seat. The accountability doesn't vanish — it's absorbed by the Solutions Engineer (client-facing governance) and the platform (automated status, templated plans, portal transparency). What stays irreducibly human is the judgment in this course: the trade-off calls, the risk reads, the hard conversations, and the go/no-go decision. Automate the reporting; never automate the accountability.
